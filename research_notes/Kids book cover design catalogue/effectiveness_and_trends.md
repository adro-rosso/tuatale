# What makes a children's picture-book cover effective and current (2023-2026): trends, buyer psychology, art-director guidance

Scope: picture books for ages 2-8, 2023-2026 focus with classics as context. Research date: 2026-10-23. Evidence (surveys, award citations, named practitioners) is separated from opinion (vendor blogs, forum posts) inline. Several high-value primary pages were blocked (SLJ, Horn Book, CBCA site, Bologna press PDF, Creative Bloq body) and are listed in Gaps.

## Key Question 1: What 6-10 principles do art directors and designers cite most for picture-book covers that sell?

### Takeaway
Across a children's-publishing art director (Laurent Linn, S&S), a 25-year picture-book maker (Helen Stephens), an SCBWI regional advisor, Reedsy's designer panel, and a trade art director (Helen Yentus, Riverhead), the recurring principles are: one large, expressive character placed front-and-centre and usually facing the reader; a cover that reads at ~2 inches/postage-stamp size; a title that is big, bold and physically integrated with the art; a palette tuned to age (saturated/high-contrast for the youngest); restraint (white space, limited fonts, no clutter); and the cover must sell to both child and adult simultaneously. No source offers a controlled test linking any single principle to sales; these are expert consensus, not measured effects.

### Cited Findings

**Principle 1 - Dual audience (child + adult buyer)**
- Reedsy's children's cover guide: covers must "speak simultaneously to the child who will read the book and the adult who will buy it." Designer Evgenia P.: simple, rounded shapes and clear focal points draw young children, while colour harmony and composition appeal to adults. — [Reedsy children's book covers](https://reedsy.com/resources/book-covers/childrens)
- Laurent Linn (art director, Simon & Schuster), via illustrator Debbie Ridpath Ohi: a cover must signal the book's genre and mood, and "encourage a young child to pick up the book, or encourage an adult to read it aloud." — [Debbie Ohi, Picture Books 101: creation of a picture book cover](https://debbieohi.substack.com/p/picture-books-101-behind-the-scenes)
- Reedsy: "Age range is the most important variable in the entire brief"; covers for 3-year-olds and 10-year-olds share almost nothing in style, typography, palette or emotional register. — [Reedsy](https://reedsy.com/resources/book-covers/childrens)

**Principle 2 - One big expressive character, front and centre, usually facing out**
- Helen Stephens (author-illustrator, "After 25 years of making picture books"): the default is that "your main character would face out of the book, looking at the reader" to create an immediate connection; facing the page-turn direction (left-to-right in the West) leads the reader in; facing the spine is usually reserved for two characters interacting (connection/friendship); "there are no hard fast rules" (e.g. the bear in Jon Klassen's *I Want My Hat Back* faces back into the story for a plot reason). Commenter Vivian French: check the image in a mirror to see if it reads better flipped. — [Helen Stephens, "Dear Helen", 26 Apr 2023](https://helenstephens.substack.com/p/dear-helen)
- Troy Cummings (author-illustrator of 30+ books) on *Can I Be Your Dog?*: a sketch was rejected because "the puppy was too small, which made the cover static"; the art director enlarged the main image, bolded the title, bent a letter of the title, and moved the byline into background space; the final jacket "reads well from across a room and as a small web thumbnail." — [Tara Lazar blog, Troy Cummings guest post](https://taralazar.com/2018/03/15/how-to-sketch-a-fetch-worthy-picture-book-cover/)
- Reedsy: expressive characters are "a common trope: large, simple figures with distinct emotions." Designer Marina S.: "Expression leads when the book is about a feeling, not a plot"; setting can hint at story without overpowering emotion. Friendship pairs and magical objects are other common tropes. — [Reedsy](https://reedsy.com/resources/book-covers/childrens)
- The Book Designer's 2023 analysis of ten strong children's covers attributes most of their pull to the protagonist's facial expression (e.g. *Sulwe*'s "curious, wonder-filled expression invites the reader in"; *Hair Love*'s tender father-daughter moment; *Alice Fleck*'s "bewildered expression in a cluttered kitchen is relatable and light in tone"). — [The Book Designer, 28 Nov 2023](https://www.thebookdesigner.com/10-best-childrens-book-covers/)
- Troubador's 2026 children's-cover trend piece lists "include a character" and "avoid clutter" among its evergreen tips, alongside "integrate cover text with the illustration." — [Troubador, Children's Book Cover Trends 2026, 9 Jan 2026](https://troubador.co.uk/blog/childrens-book-covers)
- Eye-contact/sales link: an SLJ post ("The Eyes Have It") cites only a tongue-in-cheek "unscientific" report that books with large eyes looking at passers-by circulate more; page itself was blocked (403) so quote is from the search snippet. — [SLJ, The Eyes Have It](https://slj.com/story/the-eyes-have-it-the-allure-of-books-that-look-at-you) (opinion/unverified)
- Illustrator Garry Parsons: eye rendering (dots, dots-in-circles, lids/lashes) is part of the whole character style; book markets worldwide "have differing collective preferences" on eyes, which matters for co-editions, but he names no countries and no data. — [Garry Parsons via Moira Butterfield's blog, 2018](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/17534642-looking-into-the-eyes-of-picture-book-characters) (opinion)

**Principle 3 - Thumbnail / 2-inch / squint test**
- Cathy Thole-Daniels (NJ SCBWI regional advisor): sketch at least three thumbnails in different directions, enlarge to printed size, then "check the cover at two inches" because readers first see covers at thumbnail size online: "Is the cover compelling at a small size?" Also: view the work upside-down or backwards to spot problems. — [Cathy Thole-Daniels, 8 steps to a great book cover](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/15807587-guest-post-8-steps-to-illustrating-and-designing-a-great-book-cover-ca)
- Troy Cummings draws thumbnails ~1.5 inches tall: "It forces me to work quickly, make big, bold shapes, and to not get fussy with details"; works black-and-white first, colour later. — [Tara Lazar blog](https://taralazar.com/2018/03/15/how-to-sketch-a-fetch-worthy-picture-book-cover/)
- Helen Yentus (art director, Riverhead/PRH): covers are mostly seen at "postage-stamp size on Amazon," so the team has moved toward bolder, simpler designs; subtler details can work when a physical book is held in hand. — [Penguin Random House author news](https://authornews.penguinrandomhouse.com/behind-the-scenes-designing-your-books-cover/)
- Vendor guides (opinion, no data): "if you can't read the title at 160px wide, neither can shoppers"; test at ~200x300px; squint and check whether the key shape pops; shrink to postage-stamp size and check whether the title disappears or the image turns to mush. A repeated claim that "90% of first impressions happen at thumbnail size" has no source. — [Vappingo KDP cover tips](https://www.vappingo.com/word-blog/kdp-book-cover-design-tips/); [The Author Central, common cover mistakes](https://theauthorcentral.com/blog/common-book-cover-mistakes-new-authors-make/); [KDPeasy](https://www.kdpeasy.com/guides/book-cover-design-mistakes)

**Principle 4 - Title: big, bold, legible, integrated with the art; hand-lettered reads as professional**
- Reedsy: "Title treatment matters as much as the art"; favours custom or hand-drawn display type that works with the illustration; designer Vaughan D. advises an open "a" (single-storey) for young readers; designer Evgenia P.: the title font should look unique but gentle. — [Reedsy](https://reedsy.com/resources/book-covers/childrens)
- Thole-Daniels: title should be large and easy to read, no more than two fonts; if the art is extensive, leave roughly two-thirds of the space for it. — [8 steps](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/15807587-guest-post-8-steps-to-illustrating-and-designing-a-great-book-cover-ca)
- Laurent Linn chose a handwritten "ALL" on the final *I Want to Read All the Books* cover; the title itself was changed from *All the Books* at the editor's suggestion because it better conveyed the story; layout left open space at bottom-centre for the byline. — [Debbie Ohi](https://debbieohi.substack.com/p/picture-books-101-behind-the-scenes)
- Vendor guides warn thin or hand-lettered fonts "often fail" at thumbnail size and light text on busy/pale backgrounds disappears. — [Vappingo](https://www.vappingo.com/word-blog/kdp-book-cover-design-tips/) (opinion)

**Principle 5 - Colour tuned to age; depth via colour, not black**
- Reedsy: "Palettes are saturated and high-contrast for the youngest readers, growing more nuanced as the target age climbs." — [Reedsy](https://reedsy.com/resources/book-covers/childrens)
- Laurent Linn's cover notes: "for shadows, instead of black/gray shading, you could use a rich purple/blue which will feel less oppressive"; add saturation to foreground objects to create depth; add depth so the scene looks less flat. — [Debbie Ohi](https://debbieohi.substack.com/p/picture-books-101-behind-the-scenes)
- Thole-Daniels: choose a palette that fits the story's mood; work in CMYK for print. — [8 steps](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/15807587-guest-post-8-steps-to-illustrating-and-designing-a-great-book-cover-ca)

**Principle 6 - Restraint: white space, limited palette, no clutter**
- Troubador 2026: "White-Space Winners" (e.g. *Bath Time for the Crayons*, Jeffers/Daywalt, Jan 2026; *Raindrops*, Robin Boyden; *Mo's Stick*, Fiona Lumbers): clear, calm composition keeps characters and titles from getting lost; authors are warned against overcrowding. "Limited Palettes" (e.g. *Squirrel and Bird*, *Emerald and the Sea Dragon*): a capsule of ~three bespoke colours; "show less, get so much more." — [Troubador 2026](https://troubador.co.uk/blog/childrens-book-covers)
- Vendor guides: more than 2-3 fonts or heavy effects look amateur; highly detailed renders become visual noise at thumbnail. — [The Author Central](https://theauthorcentral.com/blog/common-book-cover-mistakes-new-authors-make/) (opinion)

**Principle 7 - Cover grows out of the story; read the whole book first; research the market for a fresh angle**
- Thole-Daniels: read the entire book, note key symbols/characters; research other covers in the market "to find a fresh angle." — [8 steps](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/15807587-guest-post-8-steps-to-illustrating-and-designing-a-great-book-cover-ca)
- Saho Fujii (art director, Little, Brown Books for Young Readers): "It's important to have a working knowledge of the children's book industry and what is selling"; visit bookstores, toy/gift stores, museums, galleries; study why certain design trends work. — [Book Riot, By the Cover: Saho Fujii](https://bookriot.com/?p=103144)
- SLJ "The Cover Test" (snippet only; page 403): cover design usually begins once the interiors are nearly done so the designer understands the story; "A cover can be the best possible advertisement for the book, so it needs to accurately reflect the story." — [SLJ, The Cover Test](https://www.schoollibraryjournal.com/story/the-cover-test-winning-book-design-strategies)
- Process evidence: Linn asks for cover ideas during final interior art; multiple rounds of roughs; the illustrator relies on the art director's outside view "since the illustrator may find it hard to judge their own work." Illustrator Kelly Light's walkthrough advises: lean on the team, be patient, expect changes in designs and even the title, don't rush the final image. — [Debbie Ohi](https://debbieohi.substack.com/p/picture-books-101-behind-the-scenes); [Wheeler Studio, What ROSA Brought cover walk-through](https://wheelerstudio.com/2024/08/17/what-rosa-brought-cover-art-walk-through)

**Principle 8 - Hand-made texture as a trust signal (2024-2026)**
- Reedsy notes "a current push toward 'textured, hand-crafted looks'" which it presents as a trust signal. — [Reedsy](https://reedsy.com/resources/book-covers/childrens)
- Spine Magazine's 2026 prediction: "anti-AI, human-made covers" with visible brushstrokes, grain, noise, texture and imperfect-but-intentional lettering; craftsmanship expected to matter more. (General trade, not children's-specific.) — [Spine Magazine, trends 2025 / predictions 2026](https://spinemagazine.co/articles/book-cover-design-trends-in-2025-predictions-for-2026)

**Principle 9 - Don't talk down; humour via expression rather than gags**
- Reedsy designer warns against "infantilising or talking down to the reader." — [Reedsy](https://reedsy.com/resources/book-covers/childrens)
- Troubador 2026 "Big and Blobby": oversized blob-like characters "often with quirky expressions" on stark white or a single bright colour, with a "crazy-fun title font"; "suits small children who like bright art and amuses older readers" (e.g. *It's Not My Cookie*, Tudor/Sanders; *Rock and Roll*, Ruby Amy Thompson). — [Troubador 2026](https://troubador.co.uk/blog/childrens-book-covers)

**Principle 10 - Series/line consistency**
- Thole-Daniels: keep series covers consistent via shared imagery, style, type or layout. — [8 steps](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/15807587-guest-post-8-steps-to-illustrating-and-designing-a-great-book-cover-ca)
- Nielsen (US, 2015 study of 3,000 book-buying families): "My child asked for it" is the top purchase reason; top triggers (about half of parents) are seeing the book on the shelf, low price, and in-store impulse. — [Nielsen, Jan 2016](https://www.nielsen.com/insights/2016/why-you-shouldnt-judge-childrens-book-buyers-by-their-covers/)
- Bowker/ABC 2011 study: for ages 7-12 the main driver was a familiar character or series, then the child asking in store, then the front cover image (0-6 ranking not in snippet). — [Publishers Weekly, 2011](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/print/20110131/45943-what-do-children-s-book-consumers-want.html)

### Inferences
- The strongest common denominator is scale: character large, title large, few elements. Every process anecdote (Cummings, Linn, Yentus) moved toward bigger and bolder, never toward more detail.
- "Facing out" is the default because the cover's job is to make the adult buyer and child feel addressed; the exceptions are deliberate narrative choices, which a personalised-book pipeline should not make by accident (e.g. a randomly side-facing hero).
- Hand-lettered title + visible texture currently double as both a style and a "human-made" credibility signal, which matters more for an AI-assisted product than for a trade book.

### Gaps
- SLJ's "The Cover Test" article (the one source explicitly about testing children's covers) returned 403; only search-snippet lines are cited.
- No art director interviewed gives a numeric rule for character size (e.g. "fills 50% of the cover"); the guidance is qualitative.
- No controlled study links eye contact, expression, or character size to picture-book sales; the SLJ eye-contact piece is explicitly "unscientific."
- SCBWI/Highlights Foundation talks on covers were not found as transcripts; the Thole-Daniels post is the closest SCBWI-affiliated source.

## Key Question 2: What current (2023-2026) visual trends are visible on award lists (Caldecott, Carnegie Illustration, CBCA, Bologna Ragazzi) and bestseller/trade lists?

### Takeaway
Award lists 2023-2026 favour hand-made, mixed-media, textured work (pastel, pencil, scratchboard, paper-cut, collage, watercolour), limited or mood-driven palettes (monochrome shifting to colour, earthy/dark palettes for nature), and emotion-first abstraction over literal detail. Trade trend reports for 2026 children's covers name the same cluster commercially: "Soft Touch" hand-drawn texture, "Dark and Dreamy," "Big and Blobby" characters on white/one-colour grounds, white-space covers, limited palettes, and expansive nature. There is little direct evidence of a "retro mid-century" revival specific to children's covers; nostalgia trends are documented for adult covers.

### Inferences
- The award and trade signals converge on the same look: hand-made texture, restrained palette, big simple character or big simple nature backdrop, strong emotional read. A cover that is glossy, fully-rendered, evenly lit and detail-saturated runs against both the award aesthetic and the 2026 commercial trend list.
- "Dark and Dreamy" and earthy/dark palettes on award lists show that bright/high-contrast is not the only commercially current option for 2-8; mood palettes are now mainstream for picture books, not just for YA.
- Bestseller lists (e.g. Donaldson/Rayner, Jeffers, Jarvis) appear inside Troubador's 2026 trend examples, suggesting the trend piece was derived from upcoming frontlist from major houses rather than from independent data.

## Key Question 3: What do parents/gift buyers respond to in personalised-book covers, per reviews and surveys?

### Takeaway
No independent survey of personalised-book buyers was found; evidence is review-site text (Trustpilot, reviews.io) and vendor marketing. Reviews consistently praise seeing the child's name and face, illustration quality, thick pages and gift/keepsake framing (baby showers, first birthdays, newborn gifts); the main complaints are price, flimsy or bent covers, and too few character-appearance options. Parents are forgiving of approximate likeness when choosing from avatars, but one photo-based competitor is praised specifically for "look just like my son." Christmas data shows picture books are the single most-gifted genre in the UK.

- Nearly a quarter of UK print book purchases fall in the final eight weeks of the year; ~a fifth of Nov-Dec print purchases in 2023-24 were Christmas gifts; 5% of all-format purchases (~37m books/yr) were Christmas gifts. — [NIQ, The spirit of Christmas in the UK book market](https://nielseniq.com/global/en/insights/commentary/2025/uk-christmas-book-trends)

- Nearly two in five Christmas-gift books are for the buyer's own child; grandchildren 16%; spouse/partner 10%. Ages 0-4 is the most popular gift group (girls 15%, boys 13%). — [NIQ Christmas 2025](https://nielseniq.com/global/en/insights/commentary/2025/uk-christmas-book-trends)

### Inferences
- The things reviewers volunteer about personalised books are: the name (repeatedly), the face, illustration quality, paper/cover thickness, and the giftable moment. Cover-specific praise is rare; cover-specific complaints are about physical quality (bent, flimsy), which suggests the cover is judged first as a keepsake object (hardback, thick) and second as an image.
- Avatar-based buyers calibrate expectations to "closest option"; photo-based products are judged on "looks just like." A photo-driven product therefore raises the bar it is judged against.
- Given picture books are the top Christmas gift genre and 0-4 the top gift age, cover conventions for the gift occasion (name prominent, hardback feel, warm palette) are more commercially load-bearing for a personalised product than for a trade title.

- Mumsnet search returned no threads; Reddit is blocked to this crawler. Forum-sourced opinions on "looks like a template" are therefore absent.
- No review found explicitly says a personalised book "looked generic" or "like a template"; absence in a sample of ~3 review pages is not evidence of absence.
- No data on whether the child's name in the cover title vs only inside the book affects conversion.

## Key Question 4: What makes a cover look "generic" or "AI-generated" to buyers, and how do strong covers avoid it?

### Takeaway
The tells named by librarians, designers and readers are: style inconsistency between cover and interior (or between titles in a series), warped/odd covers, peculiar or redundant titles, generic characters and settings, type "placed on top" rather than integrated, and a kaleidoscopic over-decorated polish. Strong covers avoid this through a specific story moment, expression-led characters, visible hand-made texture and imperfect-but-intentional lettering, restraint, and a title treatment that physically interacts with the art.

### Cited Findings

**Named tells**
- Jessica Ho (teacher-librarian), CSL Journal, 11 Mar 2025, on identifying AI picture books: "a peculiar sounding title," "differing cover art styles between titles in a series or cover art that just doesn't look quite right," warped front/back covers, illustrations that don't cohere page to page, "generic characters and settings," inaccurate images left unedited, and "robotic book description." Examples: *Alice and Sparkle* (early 2023, publicly AI co-written/illustrated; critics "lambasted the illustrations for their flaws and lack of consistency"); a May 2023 catalogued book whose author had 194 Amazon titles and an AI profile image. — [CSL Journal, There are Imposters Among Us](https://journal.canadianschoollibraries.ca/?p=9706)
- Spine Magazine on AI covers: likely "elaborate, decorative, kaleidoscopic imagery, vivid colors, and intricate patterns" that "may look flat, with type placed on top rather than integrated, unless a human designer intervenes." — [Spine Magazine](https://spinemagazine.co/articles/book-cover-design-trends-in-2025-predictions-for-2026)
- The Book Designer on AI covers: they "can have the same problems as book cover templates, a resemblance to other cover art," plus copyright risk if they closely resemble existing work. — [The Book Designer, AI-generated book covers](https://www.thebookdesigner.com/ai-generated-book-covers/)
- Reader/forum opinion: AI cover art "all screams BLANDLY GENERIC"; other commenters counter that many human covers look similar too because title/author must stay legible. — [SFF Chronicles thread](https://ftp.sffchronicles.com/threads/583331/page-2) (opinion)
- Student-press opinion: an AI cover is "a glaring red flag which signals the author prioritizes speed and convenience over craft." — [HiLite](https://hilite.org/101799/lite-reads/ai-cover-art-heralds-a-downgrade-in-literature/) (opinion)
- Creative Bloq reports an AI-made children's book with "a barely legible title" found in a physical shop went viral; commenters argued publishers see children as a market "less likely to care or complain about the quality." Article body was truncated on fetch; from search snippet. — [Creative Bloq](https://www.creativebloq.com/ai/ai-art/is-ai-destroying-the-art-and-magic-of-childrens-books)
- Vendor guide on why AI covers look bad (opinion): generic composition, poor type integration, no genre fit. — [KDPBuilder](https://kdpbuilder.com/blog/why-ai-book-covers-look-bad-and-how-to-fix-them)

**How strong covers avoid it (from Q1/Q2 sources)**
- A specific moment rather than a pose: The Book Designer credits *Hair Love* (father doing daughter's hair), *Alice Fleck* (bewildered in a cluttered kitchen), *Hey Grandude!* (children gazing up at Grandude with props signalling a journey). — [The Book Designer](https://www.thebookdesigner.com/10-best-childrens-book-covers/)
- Expression leads: Reedsy designer Marina S., "Expression leads when the book is about a feeling, not a plot." — [Reedsy](https://reedsy.com/resources/book-covers/childrens)
- Hand-made texture as trust signal: Reedsy's "textured, hand-crafted looks"; Troubador's "Soft Touch" (visible pencil marks "shows the illustrator's process"); Spine's "anti-AI, human-made" prediction. — [Reedsy](https://reedsy.com/resources/book-covers/childrens); [Troubador 2026](https://troubador.co.uk/blog/childrens-book-covers); [Spine](https://spinemagazine.co/articles/book-cover-design-trends-in-2025-predictions-for-2026)
- Imperfect-but-intentional lettering, and type that interacts with the art (Linn's handwritten "ALL"; Cummings' bent letter; Reedsy's custom display type). — [Debbie Ohi](https://debbieohi.substack.com/p/picture-books-101-behind-the-scenes); [Tara Lazar blog](https://taralazar.com/2018/03/15/how-to-sketch-a-fetch-worthy-picture-book-cover/); [Reedsy](https://reedsy.com/resources/book-covers/childrens)
- Restraint: Troubador's white-space and limited-palette trends; Spine's one-colour covers with one or two motifs. — [Troubador 2026](https://troubador.co.uk/blog/childrens-book-covers); [Spine](https://spinemagazine.co/articles/book-cover-design-trends-in-2025-predictions-for-2026)
- Consistency cover-to-interior: Ho's primary tell is style mismatch; the SLJ snippet says the cover "needs to accurately reflect the story." — [CSL Journal](https://journal.canadianschoollibraries.ca/?p=9706); [SLJ Cover Test](https://www.schoollibraryjournal.com/story/the-cover-test-winning-book-design-strategies)

### Inferences
- The "AI look" is mostly the absence of decisions: no single big idea, type floating over art, evenly rendered detail everywhere, and a character in a neutral pose. The antidotes map one-to-one onto the art-director principles in Q1 (one idea, big character, integrated title, restraint).
- For a personalised product, the highest-risk tell is cover/interior style drift and series-to-series drift (Ho's first two signs), because every order is effectively a new title; locking one medium/palette/lettering system across cover and pages is the cheapest defence.
- Visible texture and hand-lettering are currently doing double duty as fashion and as provenance; both can be engineered into a pipeline (texture overlays, a fixed hand-drawn title face) without claiming hand-making.

### Gaps
- No buyer-side survey quantifies how often shoppers reject a cover as "AI-looking"; all evidence is librarian/designer/reader opinion.
- Creative Bloq body text was unavailable; the viral-example details come from a search snippet.

## Key Question 5: Is there evidence on landscape-format covers and how title placement differs from portrait?

### Takeaway
Very little. The only format-specific sources are a print-on-demand publisher's note that landscape gives picture books lateral room so text can sit under images, and a comic-cover layout guide (non-children's) suggesting a horizontal title band gives stability and keeping the title off faces. No art director source discussed landscape vs portrait title placement for picture books.

### Cited Findings
- FriesenPress: landscape "gives picture books more lateral room, so single lines don't crowd the page," and text can sit under each image. (Interior-focused, not cover-specific.) — [FriesenPress, The Subtle Magic of Landscape Format Books](https://www.friesenpress.com/blog/2019/3/6/publishing-a-book-landscape-format)
- MediBang Paint (doujinshi/comic layout guide): keep the title off faces; placing the title horizontally across the cover "can give a feeling of stability"; splitting the title at both ends lets a central figure stand out. — [MediBang, examples of cover title layout](https://medibangpaint.com/en/use/2020/09/examples-of-cover-title-layout) (general, not picture-book-specific)
- 99designs maintains a landscape book-cover inspiration gallery but offers no guidance. — [99designs landscape covers](https://99designs.com/inspiration/book-covers/landscape)
- Linn/Ohi: the portrait *I Want to Read All the Books* cover left open space at bottom-centre for the byline; Cummings' art director moved the byline "into the background space" - both examples of reserving a quiet zone for type regardless of orientation. — [Debbie Ohi](https://debbieohi.substack.com/p/picture-books-101-behind-the-scenes); [Tara Lazar blog](https://taralazar.com/2018/03/15/how-to-sketch-a-fetch-worthy-picture-book-cover/)

### Inferences
- In landscape the thumbnail is shorter, so a title stacked vertically (portrait habit) loses height; the comic-guide's horizontal band/stability note and Yentus's "postage-stamp" point together suggest a wide, single-line or two-line title across the top or bottom third, with the character occupying the opposite band, as the safer default. This is inference, not sourced guidance.
- Reserving a quiet background zone for type (Linn, Cummings) is orientation-independent and transfers directly.

### Gaps
- No art director, publisher guide or study found that addresses landscape vs portrait cover composition or title placement for picture books; this is an evidence hole.
- No data on landscape share of picture-book output or of award winners.

## Key Question 6 (supplementary): Data on covers and sales/conversion in online stores

### Takeaway
The only robust data is NielsenIQ's UK consumer survey (cover influences 13% of all purchases; children's categories incl. picture books highest; 30% when discovered in-store). No published Amazon cover A/B dataset exists; Amazon offers no native cover split test for books. One BookBub ad test showed a cover change roughly doubling click-through.

### Cited Findings
- NielsenIQ Books & Consumers (12 months to May 2024): front cover influenced 13% of all UK book purchases; 15% of print; 18% of impulse; 19% in-store; 30% of books discovered in physical shops; children's highest, incl. picture books; 20 authors had >=20% cover-influenced purchases over three years. — [NIQ, Go ahead, buy the book for the cover](https://nielseniq.com/global/en/insights/commentary/2024/go-ahead-buy-the-book-for-the-cover/)
- Amazon has no built-in A/B test for book covers/descriptions; tests must run sequentially with a baseline (a KDP guide recommends >=3 weeks baseline). — [Vappingo, A/B test Amazon book description](https://www.vappingo.com/word-blog/ab-test-amazon-book-description/)
- Publishing.co.uk describes a sequential Sponsored-Products cover test (14-30 days per cover) and warns results are "confounded by other variables"; its claim that a cover drives ~80% of clicks is unsourced. — [Publishing.co.uk, A/B testing book covers](https://publishing.co.uk/guides/ab-testing-book-covers/) (opinion on the 80%)
- BookBub ad test (not Amazon, adult series): final round CTR 0.58% original vs 1.21% new cover at 2,200 impressions each; an earlier round showed 5.2% CTR for a large-typography version. — [BookBub Insights](https://insights.bookbub.com/boosted-series-sales-testing-cover-designs/)
- Nielsen US 2015 (3,000 families): top purchase reason "my child asked for it"; top triggers seeing on shelf, low price, in-store impulse. — [Nielsen 2016](https://www.nielsen.com/insights/2016/why-you-shouldnt-judge-childrens-book-buyers-by-their-covers/)

### Inferences
- The cover matters most where discovery is visual and impulsive (in-store 30%); online, the thumbnail is the equivalent shelf, which is why every practitioner source converges on the 2-inch test.
- The BookBub result (large typography doubled CTR) is the only quantified signal that a bolder title lifts clicks; it is adult fiction and a single case.

### Gaps
- No picture-book-specific or children's-specific cover A/B or conversion data was found anywhere.
- Full-year 2024 picture-book BookScan totals (UK/US) are paywalled.
